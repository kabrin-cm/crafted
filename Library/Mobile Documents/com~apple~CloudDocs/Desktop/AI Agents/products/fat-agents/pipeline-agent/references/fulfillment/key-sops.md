# Mastering VSLs (Video Sales Letters) SOP

_Source: Mastering VSLs (Video Sales Letters) SOP.docx_

## Mastering VSLs
## (Video Sales Letter Creation) SOP
Presented By Jeremy Haynes


### Made For:
Personal Brands, High Ticket Product or Service Businesses, Digital Marketers, Entrepreneurs, and Anyone looking to scale their business using high-converting Video Sales Letters (VSLs).

### Background
Over the years, I've seen countless marketers and business owners overcomplicate the hell out of their VSLs. They get lost in templates, scripts, and end up creating these monstrosities that bore their audience to death. Let's cut the crap. If you're not using a mini webinar template or any kind of VSL-based template, there's a simple flow that works like magic every single time.
When you run a VSL and you're going off-script, or you just want to make your own template, there's a straightforward path to follow. It's not rocket science, but it does require you to be direct, honest, and to the point. This SOP is going to lay it all out for you.

### The Objective
Our goal here is simple: Create a VSL that grabs your audience by the collar, holds their attention, and compels them to take action. We're going to achieve this by following a proven flow that addresses every concern, builds trust, and eliminates any friction in the buying process.

### The Process
#### 1. Hook Them Immediately: What You Can Help Them With
Start your VSL by telling them exactly what you can do for them. Be direct. This is your opening hook, and it needs to be strong enough to make them think, "Yeah, I'm in the right place."
Example:
"Hey guys, my name is Jeremy Haynes. I can help you master internet marketing. If you're looking to get into digital marketing, I'm your guy."
No fluff, no beating around the bush. Tell them who you are and how you can solve their problem.
#### 2. Establish Credibility: Why You Can Help Them
Now that you've got their attention, you need to build trust. This is where you dive into why you're the person or company that can actually help them.
How to Do It:
Share Relevant Experience: Mention how long you've been in the game, the successes you've had, and any significant milestones.
Provide Specifics: Use concrete numbers that are believable and demonstrate your expertise.
Avoid Irrelevant Details: Don't bore them with unnecessary backstory. They don't care where you're from unless it directly relates to how you can help them.
Example:
"Allow me to introduce myself. I've been a digital marketer for over 12 years and have spent a ton of money on paid advertising. The better you are at these skills, the more money you can make. My first skill set was..."
Keep it focused on building credibility.
#### 3. Sell Them on the Opportunity: The Bigger Picture
After establishing why they should trust you, broaden the scope. Sell them on the industry or the big 'why' behind what you're offering.
How to Do It:
Highlight the Value of the Industry: Explain why digital marketing (or whatever you're offering) is so important and valuable right now.
Compare and Contrast: Show them why your solution stands out compared to other options.
Use Facts and Trends: Support your points with relevant data or trends that make your argument stronger.
Example:
"Digital marketing skills are some of the most valuable you can have today. Let me tell you why mastering internet marketing can completely transform your business and your life."
Make them see the immense opportunity they're missing out on if they don't act.
#### 4. Present Your Offer: How You Can Help Them
Now, transition into how you can specifically help them seize this opportunity. This is where you present your offer in detail.
Key Points:
State the Price Upfront: Don't dance around it. Be transparent.
Explain What's Included: Break down everything they get when they sign up or purchase.
Avoid Value Stacking with Inflated Prices: Don't make up fake high prices just to slash them. It's dishonest and kills trust.
Example:
"So here's how I want to help you get involved. My Master Internet Marketing program costs $5,000. Let me explain what comes with it. You're going to get access to seven weeks of live classes, each about three to five hours long, covering different topics each week. There are worksheets, downloads, strategies, and SOPs to help you implement everything. Plus, you'll get access to all future live classes and our active Telegram community where you can ask me questions directly."
Lay it all out. Make sure they understand the value they're getting.
#### 5. Handle Objections: Answer Their Questions Before They Ask
Before they even have a chance to object, address their most common concerns. This shows that you understand them and have thought through everything.
How to Do It:
List Common Questions: Think about what people usually ask or worry about.
Provide Clear Answers: Be straightforward and honest.
Example:
"Some of the most common questions we get are:
'Why is it $5,000?' Let me break down the value for you.
'Are there any payment plans or financing options?' Absolutely, and here's how they work.
'What's the expected timeline to get results?' Typically, our students start seeing results within..."
Tackling objections head-on builds trust and lowers barriers to purchase.
#### 6. Qualify Your Audience: Who It's For and Who It's Not For
Be clear about who will benefit most from your offer and who won't. This not only attracts the right people but also saves you time dealing with unqualified leads.
How to Do It:
Describe the Ideal Client: Be specific about the traits, circumstances, and mindset of those who will get the most out of your offer.
State Who It's Not For: Politely but firmly mention who should not apply or purchase.
Example:
"This program is for action-takers who are serious about mastering digital marketing and willing to invest in themselves. If you're not ready to put in the work or looking for a get-rich-quick scheme, this isn't for you."
Filtering your audience ensures better results for both you and your clients.
#### 7. Make a Clear Call to Action: Guide Them on Next Steps
Tell them exactly what you want them to do next. Don't assume they'll figure it out on their own.
Example:
"If you're ready to take your digital marketing skills to the next level, click the link below to apply now."
Your CTA should be straightforward and compelling.
#### 8. Provide Authentic Testimonials: Show Proof
People want to see that others have succeeded with your offer. Include real testimonials that are believable and relatable.
How to Do It:
Use Actual Screenshots: Show real messages, emails, or posts from satisfied clients.
Include Names and Photos: If possible, to add authenticity.
Avoid Over-Polishing: Don't make them look too slick; authenticity trumps production value here.
Example:
"Here's what some of our students have to say:
[Insert actual screenshots of testimonials from Telegram or other platforms, showing names and photos.]"
Make sure these testimonials highlight specific results or experiences.
#### 9. Reiterate the Value: Address Any Remaining Skepticism
For those who are still on the fence, provide additional reassurance.
What to Include:
Refund Policy: If you offer one, explain it clearly.
Repeat Key Benefits: Remind them of what they stand to gain.
Final Call to Action: Encourage them to take action now.
Example:
"We stand behind our program 100%. If you're not satisfied within 30 days, we'll give you a full refund. There's no risk to you. So, click below to get started today and transform your future."
Your goal here is to eliminate any last-minute doubts.

### Additional Insights
#### Optimizing VSL Length Based on Your Audience
Understand that different audiences require different approaches when it comes to the length of your VSL.
Affluent, Action-Taking Audiences:
Preferred Length: 2 to 10 minutes.
Reasoning: They value their time and make decisions quickly. They don't need long explanations.
Less Affluent, More Analytical Audiences:
Preferred Length: 15 to 45 minutes, sometimes even longer.
Reasoning: They need more information, reassurance, and details to make a decision.
Remember: Tailor your VSL length to the financial status and decision-making style of your target audience.
#### Analyzing Your VSL's Performance
Keep an eye on key metrics to understand how your VSL is performing.
Play Rate: The percentage of visitors who click play.
Improving It: Use compelling thumbnails, ensure your page design focuses attention on the video, and frame the expectation in your ads.
Engagement Rate: How much of your video viewers watch on average.
Analyzing Drop-Off Points: Identify where viewers lose interest and adjust your content accordingly.
Conversions: Ultimately, how many viewers take the desired action.
Use tools like Wistia to get detailed analytics on your video performance.
#### Always Disclose the Price Upfront
Being transparent about your pricing is crucial.
Builds Trust: Honesty fosters credibility with your audience.
Filters Out Unqualified Leads: Saves you time and resources.
Aligns Expectations: Avoids surprises later in the sales process.
Don't hide your prices thinking it'll get you more leads. Quality over quantity.
#### Maintaining Sales Team Morale
By providing qualified, well-informed leads, you keep your sales team's morale high.
Qualified Leads: Salespeople prefer talking to prospects who are ready and able to buy.
Better Conversions: Higher morale often leads to better performance.
Avoiding Burnout: Reduces frustration from dealing with unqualified leads.
Your sales team is a critical part of your business. Keep them motivated by ensuring the leads they get are top-notch.

## PART 1: FOUNDATIONS OF DIRECT RESPONSE VIDEO ADS

### 1. What Is Direct Response Advertising?
Direct response advertising compels a specific action from your audience. Unlike branding or “awareness” ads, direct response ads say, “Click here now,” “Schedule a call,” “Download this guide,” or “Buy now.” Everything else in the ad (the script, visuals, structure, hook) supports that singular next step.
Key takeaway: Whenever you create a direct response ad, you’re optimizing for an immediate and measurable action—whether it’s a lead form submission, a booked call, a purchase, or something else.
#### Why Video (Not Images) for Direct Response?
Video grabs attention in the feed better than static images, primarily because moving visuals and human faces are harder to scroll past.
Context & Explanation: In a 30–90 second video, you can convey urgency, credibility, and address objections far better than a single image.
Dynamic ad platforms (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube) often give more cost-effective reach to video ads, especially short-form vertical videos.

### 2. Trust the Algorithm—Stop Fighting the Platform
Modern advertising platforms (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, etc.) track tens of thousands of data points per user—for example, their interests, behaviors, online purchases, and real-time engagement. The system’s algorithmic intelligence is far beyond what you can manually optimize.
#### Key Principle: Dynamic Creative
Old Way: You create a single, specific ad combination (one piece of copy, one video, one headline) and let it run.
Modern Way: You supply multiple variations (e.g., up to 10 videos, 3–5 headlines, 3–5 pieces of body copy), then turn on “Dynamic Creative.”
Why it works: The platform automatically combines these elements and serves the best-performing variants to each user—leading to lower costs and higher conversions.
#### Short vs. Long Variations
Half Your Assets Short: e.g., a 30-second ad with minimal copy.
Half Your Assets Long: e.g., a 60–90 second ad that addresses deeper reasons or short testimonials.
Let the Platform Decide: Different user segments respond to different lengths and formats.

### 3. In Market vs. Needs Convinced
One of the biggest takeaways from the transcript is to prioritize “in market” audiences. In market means they already know they want the result you’re promising (for instance, if you sell a real estate course, they already believe real estate is a great wealth-builder; they’re just looking for the best approach or teacher).
In Market (3–4% of your total addressable audience)
Already “sold” that they need what you’re offering.
Cost to convert is lowest.
Sales cycles are shorter and friction is minimal.
Example: Someone who’s already excited about Airbnb investing but hasn’t decided whose program to buy.
Needs Convinced (30% of your market)
Know they want the general outcome (e.g., “I want a second income”), but not 100% sure your method is correct.
Need more “why” content, so cost per conversion is higher.
Higher no-show and churn risk.
Everyone Else (the cold mass)
Not on the table initially. Trying to convert them first is ultra-expensive and causes chaotic lead quality.
Core Lesson: Begin your campaign structure and messaging for in-market prospects. The messaging for someone who “already wants X” differs drastically from someone who “isn’t sure if X is the right move at all.”

### 4. The Basic Ad Flow: Hook → Reasons → CTA
All direct response video ads, no matter the length or editing style, can be boiled down to:
Hook: The first 3–5 seconds that decide whether people keep watching or scroll away.
Reasons (a.k.a. The Body): The middle portion that justifies why your viewer should trust you, want your offer, or see your solution as unique.
Call to Action (CTA): Tells them exactly what to do next, e.g., “Click the link,” “Fill out this form,” “Schedule your call,” or “Buy here.”
#### Hook: The “Bait” That Matches Real-Time Context
Speak to real-time interests: For example, if your audience is “experienced real estate investors,” start with “Already own multiple properties but want to boost your ROI without adding overhead? You’ll want to see this.”
Use environment or specificity: Example from the transcript: “You’re still riding meme coins? Let me show you why that might be a disaster—and a better alternative.”
Keep it natural: The hook must feel integrated into the content they’re already consuming.
#### Reasons: Emotional + Logical
Why This is the real substance of your ad. Answer: “Why is your approach better than alternatives?” or “Why does my audience need it right now?”
Stories & Data: Use 1–2 punchy success points or micro-case studies. Don’t overdo it with random fluff.
Trust & Familiarity: People want to know it’s “for them,” so highlight specifics (e.g., “Tons of overhead with fix-and-flips? Our method requires nearly zero maintenance!”).
#### CTA: Be Direct—And Be Clear
Example: “Go to the link below to see how we help part-time real estate investors scale faster with short-term rentals. I’ll share everything in a quick training video.”
Add a “So you can” statement: “Click below so you can watch a short VSL where I break down the steps for profitable Airbnb arbitrage.”

### 5. Environment & Visual Consistency
From the transcript, one major theme was how your ad’s visual environment should resonate with your audience’s feed. If your audience loves luxury cars, or they follow pages about boat storage or real estate, placing yourself in a relevant environment (or referencing it quickly) immediately establishes that you’re “one of them.”
Ty Lopez Example: Standing in a garage with supercars to catch the eye of “car lovers” or wealth-focused watchers.
Luxury Real Estate: Filming in front of a premium property or on location.
Professional Setting: If you’re raising capital from accredited investors, a straightforward, well-lit office with business-casual attire works best.
Remember: Don’t overdo it or fake it. Authentic is better than forced “Lambo & Mansion” vibes if that’s not your style or brand.

## PART 2: IDENTIFYING & HOOKING YOUR “IN MARKET” AUDIENCE

### 1. What Does “In Market” Mean—And Why Does It Matter?
In the transcript, Jeremy often references the 3–4% of your total addressable market who are “in market.” These people:
Already believe in the broad category or vehicle you’re selling (e.g., real estate, trading, content creation, etc.).
Don’t need to be told why the overarching industry makes sense (e.g., “Does real estate even work?” They already know it does).
Need which or who: They’re evaluating which approach or which expert to pick.
Key Advantage:
Shorter sales cycles and higher ROAS—they’re already motivated and in search of a specific solution.
Lower friction—less time “convincing” them that the fundamental opportunity is valid.

### 2. Different Types of “In Market” Segments
Not all “in market” buyers are identical. Sub-segments can exist even within that 3–4%. Consider a real estate example:
Current Short-Term Rental Investors
Already have multiple properties on Airbnb/VRBO.
Want to optimize or expand quickly.
Long-Term Rental Investors
They believe in real estate but may be curious if short-term rentals have better returns.
People with a Tax Burden
Know real estate can reduce taxes, so they’re scanning for a specific strategy or property type.
Ambitious Newcomers (But Still “In Market”)
Maybe they haven’t done a deal yet, but they’re actively searching for a program or mentor to jumpstart them—already convinced “Real Estate is the best wealth builder.”
Each segment has its own motivations, language, and circumstances. Make sure your ads speak to the specifics of each (especially the “circumstances” and “why they want more/better/faster results”).

### 3. Crafting Hooks for the “In Market” Prospect
Every direct response ad starts with a Hook. For “in market” audiences, the hook must assume they’re already sold on the broad opportunity. You only need to introduce your unique angle, approach, or story.
#### 3.1 The “You Already Know” Opener
Example: “You already know real estate is one of the safest ways to build wealth, but have you considered short-term rentals for doubling your monthly cash flow?”
Why it works: It instantly acknowledges their pre-existing belief. You’re not pitching them on “why real estate,” you’re pitching on “this method within real estate.”
#### 3.2 The “You’re Doing This, But…” Opener
Example: “You’ve already tried day trading, but it’s draining you. There’s a simpler approach to consistent returns—selling options premiums.”
Why it works: You’re highlighting a mainstream method they already know, then offering a better alternative.
#### 3.3 Circumstance Openers
Example (high earner in a 9–5): “Your job pays you six figures, but you still want an extra $3k–$5k a month, without messing up your career. Good news—I found a trading approach that fits busy professionals perfectly.”
Why it works: You reference their life circumstance—a big paycheck from an employer—yet they want more. This shows immediate relevance.

### 4. Speaking Their Language: Big-Picture vs. Micro Details
“In market” folks want the bigger result—they don’t need to be sold on the basics. They do, however, appreciate details that confirm your unique approach is:
Faster or easier than the standard approach.
More profitable or less risky than the standard approach.
Compatible with their time, lifestyle, or resources.
#### 4.1 Use “Circle Talk”
From the transcript’s anecdote on circles, triangles, and squares:
Circle People (visionaries, decision-makers) want to see the big picture—fast.
Example copy snippet: “You’ve tested the waters. You believe real estate builds millionaires. This new short-term rental method can add an extra 5-figure monthly cash flow without endless phone calls and property visits.”
Why it works: It’s broad enough to get them nodding immediately—not drowning them in tiny data points or disclaimers.

### 5. Qualifying and Disqualifying Right Inside the Ad
Because “in market” prospects are so valuable, your ad can do a bit of light gatekeeping. This helps your pixel, your ad targeting, and your downstream funnel.
Example: “If you already own at least one property and want more monthly income—this 2-minute video is for you.”
Example: “This isn’t for people who think real estate is too risky or unproven. If you’re already convinced real estate is the path to wealth, keep watching.”
Result: You attract the segment of watchers who truly resonate, and you deter time-wasters or “needs convinced” skeptics.

### 6. Real-Time Contextual Hooks (Playing to the Algorithm)
Platforms like Facebook, YouTube, and TikTok deliver ads in near-real-time based on recent user behavior. If your buyer just browsed “best real estate investing strategies” or “why day-trading sucks,” the algorithm can show them your relevant ad that calls out that scenario.
Pro tip:
Incorporate mini lines that say, “Still scrolling for the fix to your day-trading headaches?” or “Already hitting your watchlist daily and not seeing the results you want?”
The platform sees your ad is about exactly what they just engaged with, so it’s likely to serve it to them at a lower CPM, and they’re likely to watch.

### 7. Simple Exercise: Write a Quick “In Market” Hook
#### Step-by-Step
Write down your ideal buyer’s top 1–2 beliefs (about your niche) that prove they want what you offer.
Acknowledge that belief in 1–2 lines.
Hint at your unique spin or advanced approach.
Example (Fitness Coach for Busy Pros):
They already believe daily exercise is important, but they can’t find time.
“You know you need a consistent fitness routine, but 12-hour workdays keep blocking you.”
“That’s why I designed a 15-minute morning protocol that’s proven to burn more fat than most 1-hour classes.”
Boom—clear, relevant, and quickly hooking the “in market” crowd (people who already want a structured daily fitness habit).

### 8. Avoiding the “Needs Convinced” Trap (Too Early)
It can be tempting to broaden your targeting or messaging: “If you aren’t sure trading works, let me show you why stocks are a safe bet!” But that’s more “mass market” or “needs convinced.” It explodes cost-per-lead, lowers show-up rates, and spawns chaos on your sales calls.
Start with the 3–4% who are in market.
Only later—if you want to scale big—test angles for “maybe real estate is good?” or “maybe trading is good?”
Big takeaway: Master your “in market” messaging first. Exploit it to the maximum. Then expand outward.

### 9. Putting It All Together
When building your direct response video ads, keep the “in market” approach front and center:
Hook: Reference the belief or situation they already hold.
Reasons: Justify why your unique approach or system outperforms the generic “mainstream” approach.
CTA: “Tap here to learn how we do it,” or “Press below to see how to get started,” etc.
Zero time wasted convincing them that your category is valid. They already believe it—just show them you’re the best in that space.

### 10. Action Steps
Segment Out Your “In Market” Audience
If you market to multiple sub-niches (like short-term vs. long-term real estate investors), create separate copy or separate ad sets with hooks addressing each one.
Draft 2–3 Hooks That Assume They’re Sold on the Industry
For each hook, open with “Since you already [do X], I’ll show you [Y].”
Test Those Hooks in short dynamic creative videos (30–60 seconds). Compare which sub-segment or angle yields the highest lead quality.
Watch for Quality Indicators
Are your show-up rates higher? Are fewer leads ghosting? That’s a sign you’ve nailed the in-market messaging.

## PART 3: DYNAMIC TESTING & RAPID ITERATION

### 1. Why Your First Campaigns Usually Miss the Mark
Even if you’ve nailed down your “in market” demographic and created multiple hook variations, there’s still a huge risk: you might guess wrong on your best angle—or you might guess the right angle but present it in a suboptimal video format. That’s why you need a systematic approach to test multiple creative variables at once.
Key Idea:
Traditional “one ad, one guess” approach is dead.
Modern approach uses dynamic creative to let the platform pinpoint your best combo.
#### Example
Instead of one 60-second video with one piece of body copy, you might load:
4 different short videos (each ~30–45 seconds).
2–3 headlines.
2–3 body copy variants.
 Then let Facebook, Instagram, or TikTok combine them to see which synergy kills it on CPC, cost per lead, or cost per call.

### 2. Prepping Your “Hook Variations” for Launch
By now, you should have at least 2 or 3 distinct hooks aimed at your top “in market” sub-segments. (If you’re new, keep it simple—don’t overload yourself with 10 angles.)
#### Tips for Shooting the Videos
Change the environment: You might film one in your office, one outdoors, one near relevant context (e.g., if you’re a real estate coach, stand in front of a property or talk from your laptop with a property listing in the background).
Keep the “Hook → Reasons → CTA” structure tight. Aim for 30–45 seconds if you’re targeting busy pros.
Vary the vibe: One might have more “hype” editing, another might be calmer, direct-to-camera. Let the platform figure out which resonates.
#### Example Hook Variation
Hook A: “You already run short-term rentals but want bigger monthly profits without adding more overhead. Let me show you how I do it.”
Hook B: “You’ve done real estate flips; short-term rentals can be the next big jump in your monthly revenue. Here’s how.”
Hook C: “You know real estate is the best wealth-creation tool—but have you tried short-term arbitrage? Let’s talk.”

### 3. Setting Up Your Dynamic Creative Campaign
Below is a high-level breakdown if you’re using Facebook/Instagram Ads Manager (the approach is similar on other platforms).
Objective: Choose “Conversions” (e.g., “Leads,” “Scheduled Calls,” or a standard event like “CompleteRegistration” if you funnel them to a booking page).
Campaign Budget Optimization (CBO): Switch it on if you’re spending at least $100–$300/day. Otherwise, Ad Set Budget is fine at lower daily budgets.
Ad Set Level:
Targeting: If you have specific in-market audiences (e.g., custom audiences of real estate watchers, lookalikes of your best customers, or interest stacks for “Short-Term Rental,” “Airbnb,” “Real Estate Investing”), pick that.
Exclude: People who already purchased or scheduled a call.
Placements: Start with Automatic Placements or Advantage+ (Meta) if you’re not sure. If your video is shot in 9:16 vertical, you might uncheck some weird placements like right column on desktop. Let the system do the heavy lifting.
Ad Level:
Dynamic Creative: Flip that toggle on.
Upload multiple videos (we recommended 2–4).
Enter multiple headlines (2–3).
Enter multiple body copy texts (2–3).
Call to Action button: Usually “Learn More” or “Apply Now.”
You now have a test that systematically checks which combos of hook, copy, and headline crush it the hardest.

### 4. Interpreting the Early Data
#### 4.1 The Real KPIs
Cost per 3-Second View: Tells you how well the hook is grabbing attention. If one video is way cheaper at hooking watchers, that’s a strong sign.
Cost per Result (Lead, Appointment, etc.): Ultimately, you want the ad that generates your best leads at the lowest cost.
Click-Through Rate (CTR) or Link Clicks: Helpful to see if your ad is at least pulling curiosity. If CTR is abysmally low (under 0.5%), your hook or copy might be too generic.
Don’t over-optimize for vanity metrics—cost per lead or cost per scheduled call is gold. But do look at CTR or 3-second views to sense if a certain hook is bombing or hooking.
#### 4.2 Minimizing Time to Statistical Significance
You can’t decide a winner off 30 clicks or 2 leads. Pump enough budget so each variation gets a fair shot. If you’re short on daily spend, let it run longer (like 5–7 days). If you can, push $100–$300/day so you see real differences in ~48–72 hours.

### 5. Knowing When to Swap, Scale, or Scrub
Swap: If a video is getting next to no attention or a sky-high cost per lead (like 2–3x higher than your top ad), kill it or rotate in a new variation.
Scale: If you see a consistent winner (cost per result is significantly lower), then feed more budget to the entire campaign. Or duplicate that ad set with a bigger budget, letting your star assets drive the volume.
Scrub: Revisit the losers—figure out if the hook was too complicated, the copy too long, or if it just didn’t resonate with your best sub-segment.
Remember: We’re not hunting for “OK” here. We want the angle that absolutely crushes cost per lead and draws the warmest, most serious prospects.

### 6. What If All Variations Suck?
Don’t panic. If every angle bombs, one of two things is probably happening:
Wrong Audience: You might be hitting a “needs convinced” crowd. Or your interest stack is too broad and you’re not funneling out unqualified leads.
Messaging Mismatch: Perhaps you’re talking about “why real estate is great” but your in-market folks are like, “Dude, I already know that.” Or you’re ignoring the real triggers that advanced investors care about (e.g., advanced ROI metrics, minimal overhead, tax advantages).
Solution:
Re-check your short scripts. Are you calling out the actual in-market viewer?
Possibly try different audiences if your creative is strong but the people reached are irrelevant.
If your “voice” is too hype-y or too general, dial it back or refine it to the big-picture “circle talk.”

### 7. The Hammer Them Strategy (Post-Opt-In)
The transcript repeatedly references the Hammer Them approach. Here’s the short version for context:

This boosts show-up rates, shortens the sales cycle, and ensures they walk into the call warm and trusting you.
But you only do that after you find an ad variation that consistently brings in the right leads. Otherwise you’re hammering a bunch of tire-kickers that never buy. So Part 3 is about finding those quality leads first.

### 8. Rinse, Repeat, & Keep Your Edge
Once you find a successful dynamic campaign setup, don’t coast forever. Ad fatigue is real. People see the ad multiple times, and performance eventually dips.
Best Practices:
Refresh your top ad variations every 3–4 weeks (or sooner if you see cost creeping up).
Shoot new short videos—just tweak the opening line, environment, or the next layer of reasons. Keep the same structure if it’s working.
Watch for new sub-segments or seasonal angles. (Example: In real estate, the “tax season” angle might crush early in the year.)

## PART 4: ALIGNING ADS & FUNNEL FOR MAX CONVERSIONS

### 1. Why Funnel Alignment Matters
Even a world-class direct response video ad falls flat if, upon clicking, the user hits a page that confuses them, contradicts your ad’s promise, or buries the next step.
Messaging Consistency: If your ad says “Short-Term Rentals with Minimal Overhead,” your landing page must reinforce that message in the headline—instantly.
Expectation Management: If your ad addresses only advanced real estate investors, the funnel must welcome them as advanced—rather than re-explaining basic real estate 101.
Friction: Everything from your opt-in form, disclaimers, to visuals can either “feel right” (and build momentum) or spike bounce rates.
#### Real Example
If the ad says, “You already have a six-figure job—here’s a trading approach for busy professionals,” but your landing page starts with “Tired of paycheck-to-paycheck living?” you instantly break the trust you built. Users think, “Wait, that’s not me,” and they exit.

### 2. Three Core Elements to Sync
#### 2.1 The Headline
Mirroring the Hook: If your ad’s final CTA says, “Learn how we double monthly rental income,” your headline must echo that (“How to Double Your Rental Income in 90 Days—Even If You Hate Landlording”).
Short & Punchy: On direct response funnels, keep the headline to one bold promise or outcome.
#### 2.2 The Visual or Hero Image/Video
If your ad showed you on camera: Place a friendly image (or short loop video) of you in the same environment.
Avoid Stock Photos: Stock images can kill credibility. A simple photo of you or your actual subject matter is better.
#### 2.3 The Sub-Headline or Caption
Clarify the Value: E.g., “A proven short-term rental system used by over 300 seasoned investors.”
Match the CTA: E.g., “Watch this 4-minute walkthrough of how we arbitrage properties without heavy overhead.”
All three should confirm the user’s thought: “Yes—this page is exactly what the ad promised.”

### 3. Minimizing Landing Page Friction (Where You Need Volume)
If your goal is to build volume (many leads quickly) or you have a lower-ticket offer, you often want very little friction:
No Extra Steps: Link in the ad → short landing page → immediate CTA (opt-in, purchase, or watch a quick VSL).
No Long Forms: An email & name (or phone) might be enough if your aim is to collect leads cheaply.
Keep the “Extras” Tucked: If you have disclaimers or T&C, link them in the footer, not front-and-center—unless compliance rules demand otherwise.
#### Example
A webinar opt-in page for a $997 product could be as simple as:
Headline: “Master Short-Term Rentals—Without Huge Down Payments”
Sub-head: “Instantly Access This 40-Minute Training & See How Our Students Net 5-Figure Cashflow”
Opt-in box (name/email) → “Get Instant Access”

### 4. Adding Intentional Friction (For High-Quality or High-Ticket Leads)
If you’re selling $5k, $10k, or $50k+ solutions, you don’t want 2,000 random tire-kickers. Instead, you want fewer qualified calls. This is where you add friction to weed out unqualified leads.
#### 4.1 Financial Qualifier
“Our Program Is $5,000. Have you set aside at least $1,000 to get started or can access financing?”
This question ensures only those who can afford it click through.
Note: Ask it after the main pitch or in an application form.
#### 4.2 Identity or Circumstance Qualifier
If your ad specifically says “For those who already own at least 1 rental property,” ask that in the funnel: “Do you currently own a property? Yes/No.”
If “No,” either hide the scheduling link or direct them to a “Sorry, not a fit” page.
#### 4.3 Time Availabilty or Mindset Qualifier
“Are you committed to investing 4–6 hours a week for the next 90 days?”
People who say “No” might be auto-segmented out.
Caution:
Overdoing friction kills conversions.
Use only the qualifiers that truly protect your time.

### 5. The Landing Page vs. VSL Page
Whether you do a classic “short opt-in page → VSL page” or “direct VSL on page one,” the principle is the same: ensure the title, bullets, and environment line up with your ad’s big promise.
#### Two Common Layouts
Layout A (Two-step):
Page 1: Headline + Brief Copy → “Register” or “Sign Up,” capturing Name/Email/Phone.
Page 2: The VSL or “Thank you, watch this video.”
Usually used for webinars, live events, or “lead magnet” funnels.
Layout B (Direct VSL):
Single page with a video near the top, a clear CTA button: “Apply Now” or “Schedule a Call.”
Often used for call funnels ($5k–$50k+ offers), since you want them to watch your short video, fill out an application, and schedule a time.
Best Practice: If your audience is advanced or “in market,” layout B (straight-to-VSL) can be best. If you’re going for more quantity or want to capture all possible leads, layout A with a “teaser” can work.

### 6. Post-Opt-In or Post-Call Booking Page
After they opt in or schedule a call, you’re not done. The transcript references the “Confirmation Page” or “Thank-You Page,” which is a goldmine to:
Reinforce the big promise.
Hammer them with short content or testimonials to keep them warm.
Give them next steps (check your email, watch your extra training, or fill out a deeper intake form if needed).
#### Example
Headline: “You’re Booked! Here’s What to Expect on Our Call.”
Short 1–2 minute “thank you” video: “Hey, can’t wait to speak with you. Meanwhile, watch these quick success stories from clients.”
2–3 quick testimonials to prime them that your solution works.
Why It Works: You keep momentum high. They get more excited (or more certain they want to attend the call).

### 7. Specifics on Compliance and Disclaimers
Depending on your niche (finance, real estate, trading, etc.), you might need disclaimers. As Jeremy highlights, “Income claims attract the wrong crowd if used incorrectly—and can cause compliance nightmares.”
Suggestions:
Put disclaimers in fine print at the bottom or after the pitch.
Avoid or carefully word phrases like “guaranteed returns” or “100% safe.” Provide disclaimers that “past results do not guarantee future results,” etc.
If you must disclaim earlier (based on your region’s legal requirements), try a subtle text below the fold or a quick line in your VSL.

### 8. A Quick Example: From Ad to Funnel

Minimal Fields (if you want leads at scale) or Add Qualifiers (“How many properties do you own?” if you want high-quality).
VSL or instant embed video: Repeats the “2 hours a week” concept, shows some proof, invites scheduling a call or purchasing a low-ticket item.
Confirmation:
“Thank you! Watch these 2 short case studies while we finalize your seat.”
“Check your inbox for instructions—see you soon!”
Everything lines up so your traffic from that ad never feels lost or bait-and-switched.

## PART 5: HANDLING OBJECTIONS & BUILDING TRUST IN ADS & FUNNEL

### 1. Why Proactive Objection Handling Is Critical
In direct response ads, you rarely close the entire sale in the video. Instead, you get them to opt in, book a call, or purchase. But if a buyer sees your ad or funnel and thinks:
“This might be too expensive.”
“I’ve tried something like this and it didn’t work.”
“I don’t have time for this.”
…and you don’t handle those thoughts, they quietly exit. Remember, they won’t book a call if they assume it’s a waste of time.
Solution: Tackle the top 2–4 objections in the ads (briefly) and in the funnel (brief or expanded, depending on the step).

### 2. Identifying Your Top Objections
#### 2.1 Gather From Sales Calls
Talk to your closers or check call recordings:
Which concerns pop up right before the buyer pulls the trigger or says “No thanks?”
If you’re solo, reflect on your last 10–15 calls—write down repeated themes.
#### 2.2 Use the “Prevention” Mindset
Ask yourself: What would a rational, busy prospect say to shut this down immediately? Examples:
“I’ve seen this ‘overnight success’ stuff before—total scam.”
“I don’t want to stare at charts or be on calls all day—this can’t be for me.”
“I’ve got no capital to invest.” (in real estate offers)
“Is it still worth it if the market dips?” (trading or investing)
Once you’ve got them (2–4 main ones), plan how to calmly address them on camera and in funnel copy.

### 3. Objecting to the Objection: Two Key Approaches
#### 3.1 Directly Refute with Logic & Examples
Example: “Don’t worry if you lack time. I built this to function in under 2 hours a week because I had a full-time job myself. It’s the entire reason I created a simpler system.”
Accompany with a quick story: “That’s how my client Jane kept her 9–5 but still netted an extra $3k a month.”
#### 3.2 Weave It into a Quick Story
Example: “You might think, ‘Isn’t real estate cyclical? What if the market crashes?’ Well, Sam hopped in last year when everyone said it was risky, and he’s already on his second property—both profitable. So while others waited, he found deals at discount.”
Pro tip: Try to keep each objection handling under 20–30 seconds in a short ad. Don’t do a 5-minute deep dive. Instead, you aim to reduce friction, not have a full debate.

Credibility/“Is This a Scam?”

CTA: “See for yourself if I’m legit. Grab this free case study below.”

## PART 6: SCALING YOUR BEST ADS FOR MAX ROI

### 1. What Does “Scaling” Mean Here?
Scaling simply means increasing your ad budget and traffic to multiply the leads or calls you’re already generating. However, more money thrown at a campaign doesn’t automatically mean more profit; in fact, cost per lead (CPL) or cost per call often creeps up if you do it haphazardly.
Goal: Maintain (or only slightly increase) your cost per result while boosting daily spend for a bigger overall net profit.

### 2. Check You’re “Actually Ready” to Scale
#### 2.1 You Have a Verified “Winner”
From previous steps, you should have:
A proven hook in your short video(s) that yields a stable cost per lead or call.
A funnel that fits that angle (i.e., your bounce rate is acceptable, your show rate or purchase rate is decent).
#### 2.2 You Can Handle the Volume
Does your sales team or your own schedule have the capacity to handle, say, double the calls?
If it’s an online purchase, can your support or fulfillment handle the extra volume?
If not: fix that first. Scaling leads while the back-end is shaky can crush morale and bloat no-show rates.

### 3. ABO vs. CBO for Scaling
In Meta (Facebook/Instagram) Ads, you have two main budget allocation systems:
#### 3.1 ABO (Ad Set Budget Optimization)
You set a specific budget per ad set.
Good for precise control—e.g., you can keep your best ad set at $500/day and new test sets at $50/day without them starving each other.
Downside: More micromanagement. If you have 10 ad sets, you must manually watch and tweak each budget.
#### 3.2 CBO (Campaign Budget Optimization)
You set one budget at the campaign level, letting Facebook automatically distribute it across ad sets.
Good for letting the platform push more money to the top-performing ad sets dynamically.
Downside: You lose some fine-grained control, and sometimes the algorithm might “neglect” certain ad sets, even if you wanted to test them.
Rule of Thumb:
At lower total spend (under ~$300/day), ABO can give you more control over distribution.
As you scale (over $500–$1,000/day), CBO can be more efficient, letting the algorithm optimize in real-time.

### 4. Gradual vs. Aggressive Budget Increases
#### 4.1 Gradual (10–20% Increase Every 2–3 Days)
The “safe” approach. Facebook’s learning phase isn’t jolted too hard, so cost per lead remains stable.
Example: You spend $100/day. Increase to $120/day for 2 days. If cost per result is still good, go to $150, etc.
#### 4.2 Aggressive (Doubling or More)
If your funnel is super stable—like you’re 100% sure it can handle double the volume—go for it.
Risk: The platform re-enters a “learning phase,” and your cost might spike for a few days before it stabilizes.
Recommendation: If you see consistently low cost per lead/call for 5–7 days, test a more aggressive jump. But watch daily performance closely.

### 5. “Scale by Duplication” Method
Some advertisers prefer duplicating a winning ad set/campaign instead of just upping the budget inside one campaign. Why?
Fresh Learning: The new duplicate ad set starts its own learning phase, sometimes capturing a different audience pocket.
Budget Control: You can keep the original “winner” running at the same budget (to avoid messing it up) while testing a bigger budget in a new copy.
Potential Downside:
The new duplication might create overlap with the old campaign, especially if the same audience is used. But if you have a large enough audience (millions) or are targeting broad, it’s rarely an issue.
Use whichever approach (simple budget raises vs. duplication) you find more reliable. Monitor the results.

### 6. Watch for Signs of “Scale Fatigue”
As you increase spend:
CPL (Cost per Lead/Call) creeps up 20–30% or more.
Facebook Ad Relevancy or frequency metrics show the same audience being hammered.
Sales team feedback: “We’re seeing leads who are less qualified.”
If performance flattens or declines significantly, stop or lower the budget. Possibly bring in new hooks, fresh videos, or expand your audience strategy.

### 7. Managing the “Audience Ceiling”
If your entire audience is ~1 million people, you can typically scale up to a certain daily spend (maybe $1k–$2k/day) before you saturate that group. Once you saturate:
Costs spike.
Frequency soars (people see your ad repeatedly).
Quality plummets because it’s showing to the outer edges of that interest group.
#### Solutions
Broaden your targeting if you’re artificially narrowing it. Let the algorithm find fresh pockets.
Create new lookalikes or retargeting expansions.
New angles: a new hook can re-ignite interest from segments of the same audience that ignored your old angle.

### 8. Refreshing Creatives While Scaling
Even if your existing ad is a winner, consistent performance can degrade over weeks. So you:
Keep the best “hero” ad going.
Add 1–2 new variations with a different hook or a slight spin.
Rotate them in the same campaign or new ABO sets.
Compare them to your hero ad’s cost per lead. If they’re close or better, keep them. If not, kill quickly.
Result: You maintain stable volume plus avoid “ad fatigue.”

### 9. Scale in Waves
Sometimes you push from $100/day to $300/day, your cost remains stable. Hold that for a couple of days to gather enough leads and calls for your team.
Then push from $300/day to $500/day. Evaluate again. This wave approach ensures you aren’t overwhelmed on the back-end, and your cost data remains clear at each stage.

### 10. Action Steps for Part 6
Identify a campaign that’s proven stable for at least 5–7 days.
Decide ABO or CBO strategy. For bigger budgets, try CBO.
Increase budget by 20–50% (or up to 2x if you’re confident), watch 2–3 days. If performance holds, keep pushing.
Monitor audience size, frequency, cost per result. If cost rises too much, try fresh creative or open new audiences.
Do it in waves: Each wave of scale, let the system adapt, handle calls/leads, check show-up and close rates, then expand more if you want.

## PART 7: ADVANCED AD MESSAGING & KEEPING CREATIVES FRESH
Now that you’ve successfully scaled your best ads, it’s time to ensure they don’t go stale. Even the most profitable hook eventually declines in performance if you never switch it up. This section covers how to iterate on your ads—tweaking the hook, environment, or proof—so you can continue capturing new attention and maximizing ROI.

### 1. Why You Can’t “Set and Forget” Your Ads
Ad Fatigue: After a few weeks (or months), the audience sees the same visuals or hears the same opening line, and performance gradually dips.
Audience Changes: People’s interests or financial situations evolve. If you keep the same creative forever, you miss new pockets of in-market leads.
Rising Costs: As performance slips, your cost per lead/purchase creeps up—cutting profit.
Solution: Regularly refresh the hook or creative style while keeping your proven formula intact.

### 2. Rotate Beyond Your “Majority Hook”
From earlier parts, you likely found a “majority hook” that outperforms everything else. Over time, you’ll want to test:
Second-Order Hooks:
Not quite as powerful as your #1 angle, but still resonant.
Example (real estate): “Earn 2–3x a normal rental’s monthly cash flow with short-term rentals.”
Minority Hooks:
Smaller, niche angles.
Example: “Use short-term rentals as a strategic way to cut your tax bill.”
By layering in these sub-hooks, you’ll reduce ad fatigue and potentially discover a new majority hook that can scale again.

### 3. Keep the Core Flow, Vary the First Seconds
#### 3.1 The Power of “New Openers”
You can keep the same reasons/CTA (the core “body” of the script) while changing:
Opening line: “Tired of [pain]?” → “Still stuck with [pain]? Let’s fix that.”
Environment: Office vs. living room, outdoors near a property, or a simple walk-and-talk style on your phone.
Result: Audience sees it as a “brand new” video, but you’re not reinventing the wheel.
#### 3.2 Minimal Edits with Maximum Freshness
Add text overlays or progress bars.
Cut out fluff from your old 60s version to make it a 30s “quicker” version.
Re-order a line or two (e.g., handle a major objection 10 seconds in instead of 25 seconds in).

### 4. Updating Proof & Testimonials
Fresh success stories or bigger numbers (e.g., you used to say “400 students,” now it’s “600”) can reinvigorate the same ad structure:
Mention new data in the body: “Now we’ve helped 800 people do this.”
Insert a snippet of new testimonial footage or a new screenshot.
Anchor the updated credibility: “We’re adding about 50 new success stories a month—here’s a quick look at the latest.”
Tip: Don’t bury watchers in a full 2-minute testimonial. Just 5–10 seconds of “Hey, it worked for me” can do wonders.

### 5. Using Seasonal or Topical Twists
Sometimes an external event or trend gives you a quick new angle:
For Q4: “Cap off your best year in real estate with this short-term rental strategy.”
Market Volatility: “The market’s in chaos, but my approach stands firm—here’s how.”
Even a 1-sentence mention at the start of the video can keep it feeling current—while 80% of the script remains the same.

### 6. Pulling Insights from Sales Calls
Your closers (or you, if you’re the closer) hear fresh phrases from prospects daily. This is gold for new short ads:
Direct Quote: “A lot of people tell me: ‘I don’t have time for day trading. Any simpler approach?’ That’s exactly why I made this system…”
Specific Fear: “Half our leads asked, ‘What if the market crashes next year?’ Let me tackle that in 30 seconds here…”
Key: Real prospects’ language resonates more than your internal marketing speak.

### 7. Testing Your Tweaks Quickly
Duplicate your best dynamic creative campaign.
Swap the new footage or revised openers in place of the old.
Keep a couple original winning variations as “control” versions.
Let it run for 3–5 days with moderate budget. Compare cost per lead/call.
If new angles do ~as well or better, great—scale them. If not, revert to your old set but keep iterating monthly.

### 8. The Micro-Splice & “Pattern Interrupts”
Just re-editing the same raw footage can create a “new” ad:
Shorter Cuts: Chop a 45s video into a 25s version, cutting fluff.
Pattern Interrupt: Insert comedic or abrupt visuals 5s in, or highlight text to jolt scrollers.
Change Font/Colors: The same message with bigger, bolder text overlays might drastically improve thumb-stopping power.
You can keep the same script but re-package it for fresh engagement.

### 9. Maintaining a “Creative Calendar”
#### 9.1 The 2–4 Week Cycle
Every 2–4 weeks, check if your cost per lead/call is creeping up.
If yes (or you suspect ad fatigue), plan 1–2 new angles or a simple refresh: new environment, updated proof, or seasonal mention.
#### 9.2 Batch Shooting
Film 2–4 short videos in one session, each 30–60 seconds.
This stash can keep your ad library rolling for a month or more.

### 10. Action Steps for Part 7
Pick 1–2 second-order hooks or new angles.
Refresh your top ad with a changed opening line, environment, or updated proof.
Launch a test in dynamic creative, keeping a best performer as a control.
Check performance in 3–5 days. If stable or improved, scale. If worse, revert and try the next idea.

## PART 8: EXPANDING YOUR DIRECT RESPONSE AD FRAMEWORKS

### 1. Why Multiple Frameworks Matter
While you might have one go-to format for your direct response videos (short monologue camera-facing, for instance), there are other effective frameworks that can:
Capture new attention from different angles.
Prevent creative fatigue by varying the feel and style of your ads.
Serve different sub-segments of your audience who respond better to, say, a “demo style” vs. a “case study style.”
Bottom line: By rotating frameworks, you keep your messaging fresh without inventing an entirely new pitch.

### 2. Classic “Talking Head” + B-Roll Overlay
#### 2.1 The Approach
You speak directly to the camera in a single shot.
Midway through, you overlay relevant B-roll (footage of you working, site visits, or quick text screens showing results).
Return to talking head for the CTA.
#### 2.2 Why It Works
Personal Connection: They see your face, your expressions—builds trust.
Variety in Visuals: The B-roll offers a pattern interrupt so viewers don’t get bored.
#### 2.3 Basic Sequence
Hook (talking head, direct camera)
Reasons with B-roll segments (e.g., showing results, homes, charts, etc.)
Return to Head for CTA.

### 3. “Case Study Snapshot” Ad
#### 3.1 Structure
Hook: “How did Tom go from X to Y in just 3 months?”
Snapshot: Quick bullet or short footage of Tom praising your method or showing his “before vs. after.”
CTA: “Watch the same method Tom used. Click below.”
#### 3.2 Why It Works
Social proof is front and center, and the hook is a real person’s story.
You can avoid “making it all about you” by shining the spotlight on a student/client.
Tip: Keep the “Case Study” portion short—15–30 seconds. Don’t let it become a full-blown testimonial. You still want to highlight the main direct response angle and CTA.

### 4. Demo or “Screencast” Framework
#### 4.1 Perfect for Tech or Step-by-Step
Hook: “3 steps I took on my laptop to earn an extra $3k a month.”
Demo: Show your screen (or a simplified phone screen) doing the key steps.
CTA: “Ready to replicate this? Click to see the detailed version.”
#### 4.2 Why It Works
If your audience loves quick “how-to” glimpses, seeing an actual process on-screen convinces them it’s real.
This can be done with simple screen-recording + voiceover, or filming yourself while toggling between screen shots.

### 5. “Objection First” Framework
#### 5.1 Approach
Flip the usual script: start with the biggest objection as the hook.
Hook: “You think short-term rentals require massive time or capital? Let me prove you wrong.”
Body: Address that single objection in 15–20 seconds, showing how your method counters it.
CTA: “Interested? Click now to see the details or schedule a call.”
#### 5.2 Why It Works
Immediately tackles the #1 reason they’d scroll away.
If you resolve it convincingly, they’ll keep watching to see your solution.

### 6. “Question & Answer” Style
#### 6.1 Setup
Another angle: Begin by reading or stating a user’s question.
Hook: “A lot of people ask me, ‘Aren’t fix-and-flips riskier now?’ Let’s answer that.”
Body: Provide a short, direct reply. Possibly use a quick anecdote or success snippet.
CTA: “Got more questions? I answer them in my free mini-training—click below.”
#### 6.2 Why It Works
The Q&A style feels organic, as though you’re responding to real inquiries.
Great for warm audiences, but also resonates with cold if the question is common enough.

### 7. 2-Person “Interview” or “Podium” Style
#### 7.1 The Flow
Interviewer (friend, colleague, or actor) lobs questions: “You claim short-term rentals can double monthly net—explain that.”
You answer quickly, weaving your direct response points.
CTA: Interviewer might say, “So where can folks learn more?” You respond with the link.
#### 7.2 Why It Works
Breaking the “one-person monologue” pattern.
Feels more “credible” if the interviewer is half-skeptical or clarifies the method from an everyday standpoint.
Note: Keep it short—30–60 seconds total. You don’t want a 5-minute interview ad. Micro-bites.

### 8. Skit or “Scenario” Framework
#### 8.1 The Outline
A quick role-play or comedic scenario that underscores your pitch.
Hook: “Meet Sarah. She’s done with chaotic day-trading that robs her mornings.”
Show Sarah fumbling or complaining, then you appear with a “solution.”
CTA: “If you feel like Sarah, watch how we solved it in minutes.”
#### 8.2 Why It Works
Humor or mini-story stands out among typical feed ads.
If done succinctly, it cements your solution as the “hero moment.”
Warning: This can flop if it’s too cheesy or long-winded. Keep it tight.

### 9. Blending Frameworks in Dynamic Creative
Since your platform likely supports multiple ad variations in a single campaign (via dynamic creative), you can:
Upload a talking-head angle, a Q&A snippet, and a case-study snippet.
Let the platform test which resonates best with each viewer.
Watch results. The platform might push the “Skit” style to certain subgroups, the “Talking Head + B-Roll” to others.
Result: You’re effectively using multiple frameworks at once to see what hits your ideal audience best.

### 10. Action Steps for Part 8
Pick at least 1 new format from the above list (like Q&A or a micro case study) to produce as your next ad variation.
Keep your proven “Hook → Body → CTA” structure. Just shift the “Body” to reflect the new style.
Film or edit it short (30–60 seconds max) to stay direct.
Test it in a dynamic campaign with your existing best performer to see if the new framework can match or beat your current cost per lead/call.

## PART 9: STREAMLINING YOUR AD CREATION WORKFLOW
Your SOP so far covers scripting, hooks, and different frameworks. Now we’ll ensure you can produce these ads on a regular schedule—without overwhelming yourself or a small team.

### 1. Why a Defined Workflow Matters
Consistency: The direct response ad game thrives on fresh creative every 2–4 weeks.
Efficiency: Having a set “who does what” and “when we shoot/edit” prevents delays.
Scalability: As you grow, you might add more angles and frameworks—an organized process keeps quality high.

### 2. Example Ad Creation Team Roles
Whether you’re a solo operator or have a mini-team, the roles stay the same—just sometimes a single person wears multiple hats.
Copywriter/Scripter
Drafts or refines the Hook → Body → CTA.
Incorporates new angles, any fresh stats or testimonials.
On-Camera Talent
You or a spokesperson who can deliver lines confidently.
Possibly includes a second person for Q&A or interview frameworks.
Videographer or Editor
Handles filming or phone-camera capturing.
Edits for short, punchy videos—adding text overlays or B-roll.
Ad Manager (Uploader)
Actually places the final ad in the platform (Facebook, TikTok, YouTube).
Sets up dynamic creative, checks compliance, picks headlines, etc.
#### Solo? No Problem
If you’re alone: schedule “script day,” “shoot day,” and “edit day” or outsource editing.
Track your tasks in a simple doc or project management tool.

### 3. Pre-Production: Scripting & Planning
#### 3.1 Brainstorm & Script Outline
List your new hook(s), the main reason(s), and the CTA.
Decide the framework: e.g., “Objection-first,” “Case study snippet,” “Talking head + B-roll,” etc.
Write a brief bullet script or exact lines—whichever you prefer.
#### 3.2 Gather Props or Visuals (If Needed)
If you plan B-roll, decide what you’ll shoot (screens, property visits, quick desk shots).
For an interview format, prep 2–3 questions.
Key: Keep it minimal. Overcomplicating sets or props can stall you.

### 4. Production: Recording the Ad
Allocate a short block of time (1–2 hours).
Film multiple variations in one session:
Variation 1: Standard talking head (1 environment).
Variation 2: Slightly different opener, same environment.
Variation 3: Possibly a second environment or angle if time permits.
Keep Takes Short: If you fluff a line, simply re-do quickly. Overly polished can feel less authentic.
Pro Tip: For phone filming, ensure stable lighting, minimal background noise, and hold the phone at eye level or use a simple stand.

### 5. Post-Production: Editing & Polishing
#### 5.1 Editing Basics
Cut out long pauses or stumbles.
Insert text overlays for your main points or an important stat.
(Optional) Add B-roll for ~30–50% of the video if relevant.
#### 5.2 Tools
Mobile Apps: InShot, CapCut if you’re doing it from your phone.
Desktop: Final Cut Pro, Premiere Pro, or DaVinci Resolve. Keep it simple.
Remember: 30–60 seconds is your sweet spot for most direct response ads.

### 6. Approval & Finalizing
If you have a small team:
Copy Check: Make sure disclaimers or claims align with compliance.
Talent Check: On-camera person or interviewer might want to watch the final cut.
Manager Check: The ad manager ensures the final file is correct, short, and name-labeled for easy uploading.
Solo: Do a quick final watch to confirm it’s on-message, no embarrassing mistakes.

### 7. Upload & Dynamic Creative Setup
Once you have, say, 2–3 new variations:
Name them distinctly (e.g., “[OfferName]_CaseStudyHook_V1”).
Upload to Facebook Ads Manager (or other platforms) with dynamic creative toggled on.
Choose 2–3 headlines, 2–3 copy variations, and these new videos in the same ad set.
Publish and watch performance over ~3–5 days.

### 8. The 2–4 Week Cycle
Repeat the entire process every 2–4 weeks or whenever:
Costs creep up.
Your sales team needs a fresh angle for new sub-demographics.
You want to test a new approach or hook.
Consistent short filming sessions plus quick editing is the secret sauce to an ongoing ad pipeline.

### 9. Batch Shooting & Re-Edits
#### 9.1 Batch Shooting
Once a month, block half a day to film 4–6 short ads.
Each might have a different hook or environment tweak.
You’ll have enough new creative for 4–6 weeks.
#### 9.2 Re-Edits
If an existing ad is strong, just re-edit the first 5 seconds or change the B-roll.
“New” enough for the platform to treat it differently, but no heavy lifting for you.

### 10. Action Steps for Part 9
Create a simple ad production checklist:
Script → Film → Edit → Approve → Upload.
Book monthly or bi-weekly slots in your calendar for ad filming.
Decide which frameworks (Part 7) you’ll produce this cycle.
Plan your post-production approach (who or what tool).
Publish systematically, track results, and do it again in 2–4 weeks.

If you have an older demographic and you see in your data that a majority of your users are still viewing on desktop you can likely leverage longer subject lines to increase your chances of an opening.
In the digital age, personalization isn't just a luxury—it's a necessity. By weaving in elements like a recipient's name or location right into your subject lines, you unlock a higher level of engagement.
This approach, powered by what we'll call "Personal Touch Tags," boosts open rates significantly. They shine particularly bright when integrated with targeted outreach efforts, such as celebrating customer milestones or reinforcing connections post-purchase.
Be direct and concise with subject lines. While catchy seasonal phrases catch the eye, they often fall short of engaging the mind. Instead of leaning on "Falling into discounts" or "Hot summer steals," why not spell out the real value your audience will get? Highlighting the tangible benefits of your offer or pinpointing specific deals can turn a bland subject line into a compelling invitation.
In our fast-paced world, where emails are often skimmed on the go, less is more. Aim for subject lines that are concise yet impactful—no more than 9 words and 60 characters. This ensures your message is digestible at a glance, especially on the smaller screens of mobile devices.
Our investigations into email efficacy suggest a golden rule: keep your punctuation under control. A limit of three punctuation marks per subject line helps maintain clarity and professionalism. Overdoing it can inadvertently send your email into the spam abyss, especially with an overzealous use of special characters. I generally send without any punctuation and prefer lower case sentencing for max open rates surprisingly.
I hate using emojis in subject lines.
Emojis can add a splash of color and emotion to your subject lines, making them stand out in a crowded inbox. However, moderation and strategic use are key. Stick to a single emoji to complement your message, not overshadow it. Remember, emojis can appear differently across various devices, so it's crucial to preview how they'll look to ensure your message stays clear.
To truly hit the mark with your subject lines, a bit of research and testing goes a long way. Utilize tools designed to refine your approach, from analytics on past performance to A/B and multivariate testing, which can reveal the preferences of your specific audience. By experimenting with different subject line versions, you'll discover what resonates best, ensuring your emails not only reach inboxes but also spark interest.
For an extra layer of insight, reviewing the open rates of your recent subject lines can illuminate what captures your audience's attention, guiding your strategy moving forward. ## Why People Open Emails
Most marketers would think that the main reason someone is going to open an email is because of the subject line, that’s simply not true as the dominating reason.

We surveyed the email lists of clients who would allow us to do so and then compiled the data into the visual below. The dominating reason people in your list open is because they are expecting it.


“Expecting It,” means they opted into something expecting an access email or email with whatever they opted in for. They contacted customer support and are expecting an email response back. They replied to an email for order support or with a question and are expecting a response.

This was the most dominant reason and after getting all the data back it also made the most sense. The follow up dominating reason that came in 2nd place was because the email came from a known sender.

Known senders have a higher likelihood of being opened, which also makes a lot of sense. When you shop with companies you love, you have a higher chance of opening any email they send because you love that company and work with them frequently. Same for brands or companies you support or newsletters you’re opted into and want to read.

Unknown senders would be classified as brands or companies that they have not heard from before, and are not expecting an email from.

Subject line came in 3rd place, and the preview text trailed after in a well behind 4th place with “offer” coming in last place.

With this data I’d encourage you to be thinking, “how can I get all of my emails to be expected.” That generally happens after someone knowingly opts in and is actively waiting for what you’re going to send.

We do this with “email options” which give leads the ability to select different options for what they want to see next, which also helps with further segmentation.

We’ll say something in most emails that are represented by buttons or links the recipient can select for what type of email they’d like to see next. Content, top videos of the week, newsletter content, offers/sales/discounts, more of the same, and whatever else is applicable to your company that leads might actually want from you that’s valuable to them.

## Provide actual value
In an era where inboxes are inundated with endless streams of "buy now" and "limited time offer" emails, standing out requires a shift in strategy. It's not about bombarding your audience with purchase prompts; it's about delivering real, tangible value. This section is dedicated to the art of providing actual value through your emails, not just relentless sales pitches. Particularly, we're talking about value that's tailored and contextual, rooted deeply in what you know about your recipient.
Why Value Trumps Sales Pitches
Imagine waking up, checking your email, and finding a message that speaks directly to a challenge you're facing or an interest you've been nurturing. That's the kind of email that doesn't just get opened; it gets saved, shared, and acted upon. Providing value creates a bond between your brand and your audience. It transforms your emails from interruptions into anticipated communications.
How to Provide Value
Educational Content: Share insights, tutorials, and how-tos that align with your audience's interests and needs. If you're a financial services company and you know a segment of your audience is new homeowners, send them a guide on managing home-related expenses or maximizing their investment.
Personalized Recommendations: Use the data at your disposal to offer personalized product suggestions, articles, or services. If someone has been browsing winter coats on your e-commerce site, a timely email with a curated selection of coats, along with care tips for winter apparel, can be incredibly effective.
Exclusive Content: Offer content that's exclusive to your email subscribers. This could be early access to sales, a sneak peek at new products, or content created specifically for your email list, like an in-depth industry report.
Interactive Elements: Incorporate polls, surveys, or quizzes that not only engage but also provide you with more insights into your subscribers' preferences. This interaction adds value by making subscribers feel heard and offering them content more aligned with their interests.
Behind-the-Scenes Looks: Give your subscribers a peek behind the curtain. Share stories about how products are made, introduce them to team members, or take them on a virtual tour of your office or manufacturing facility. This humanizes your brand and builds a deeper connection.
Context is Key
The golden thread that ties all these strategies together is context. The more you know about your subscribers—be it through their purchase history, engagement with previous emails, or information they've willingly shared—the more tailored and impactful your value-driven emails can be. Context transforms generic advice into "This was written for me" moments.
Start Small, Think Big
You don't have to overhaul your email strategy overnight. Start by segmenting your list and introducing one or two types of value-driven content. Monitor the engagement and iterate based on feedback. Remember, the goal is to build a relationship with your subscribers that feels personal, valuable, and rewarding.
By prioritizing value over sales, you're not just avoiding the spam folder; you're earning a spot in the daily lives of your audience. And when you've provided enough value, making a sale becomes a natural next step, not a leap of faith. This approach isn't just good email marketing; it's good business.
## Understanding what email service providers want
Navigating the world of email marketing isn't just about crafting messages that resonate with your audience; it's equally crucial to understand the gatekeepers of the digital inbox: the email service providers (ESPs). These digital sentinels play a pivotal role in determining whether your carefully crafted emails reach the inbox or languish in the spam folder. This section uncovers the often-overlooked desires of email service providers and how aligning with their expectations can catapult your email marketing success.
Deliverability Is King
At the heart of what ESPs want is deliverability. They're tasked with the Herculean effort of filtering out the chaff while ensuring that valuable, wanted content reaches the user. To achieve this, they scrutinize sender reputation, engagement rates, and the quality of content being sent. Your mission? To prove that your emails are not just wanted but welcomed.
Quality Over Quantity
ESPs are champions of quality. They prioritize senders who provide high-value content over those who bombard users with a high volume of low-quality emails. The mantra here is simple: send less, but send better. Quality content that engages and delights is your ticket to the inbox.
Engagement Drives Favor
Engagement metrics are like the pulse of your email marketing efforts, indicative of how well your content resonates with your audience. Open rates, click-through rates, and the frequency of interactions are all scrutinized by ESPs. High engagement rates signal to ESPs that your emails are valuable to recipients, thereby boosting your sender reputation and inbox placement.
Authenticity and Transparency
ESPs value transparency and authenticity from senders. This means clear "from" information, straightforward subject lines, and easy-to-find unsubscribe options. Misleading or deceptive practices are not just frowned upon; they're penalized. Being upfront and honest in your emails fosters trust not just with your audience but with the ESPs as well.
Consistency Is Key
Consistency in sending frequency and volume helps ESPs recognize and trust your sending patterns. Erratic sending behavior can trigger alarms, suggesting potential spam activity. Establish a consistent email schedule that aligns with your audience's expectations and stick to it.
List Hygiene Practices
Maintaining a clean email list is non-negotiable in the eyes of ESPs. Regularly removing unengaged subscribers, correcting misspelled email addresses, and managing bounce rates not only improves your engagement metrics but also signals to ESPs that you're a conscientious sender committed to maintaining a healthy email ecosystem.
Adherence to Regulations
Compliance with email regulations like CAN-SPAM, GDPR, and others is a clear indicator to ESPs that you're a serious and responsible marketer. These regulations are designed to protect consumers, and adherence to them is a direct reflection of your commitment to ethical email practices.
Feedback Loop Utilization
Many ESPs offer feedback loops, a mechanism that notifies senders when recipients mark their emails as spam. Utilizing these feedback loops to adjust your strategy is a proactive way to maintain a positive relationship with ESPs and improve your overall email program.
Crafting Your ESP-Friendly Strategy
Understanding and aligning with the desires of email service providers is a strategic imperative. It's about playing by the rules of the digital inbox, ensuring your emails are crafted with quality, engagement, and transparency at their core. By doing so, you're not just ensuring your emails reach the inbox; you're fostering a positive, long-term relationship with both your audience and the gatekeepers who stand between you and them.
In essence, respecting what ESPs want is a critical component of your email marketing strategy. It ensures your messages are delivered, read, and acted upon, ultimately driving the success of your digital marketing efforts. Remember, in the realm of email marketing, success is a shared journey with your audience and the email service providers that connect you.

# DSL - Deck Sales Letter Funnel Strategy

DSL - Deck Sales Letter Funnel Strategy
Presented By Jeremy Haynes of Megalodon Marketing & Josh Troy of Wires From Strangers



Made For...
Personal Brands, Ecommerce Stores, Lead Gen Businesses, Service Businesses, Funds and firms raising money, and Anyone with High Quality Social Media Content That Wants To Generate dozens or hundreds of qualified booked calls per day that your sales team will reach out to close.
Overview…
The DSL Funnel Strategy uses the mini webinar 1.0 or 2.0 embedded presentation into the application page in place of a traditional VSL (video sales letter) funnel. This strategy addresses the low play rates of traditional VSL funnels by replacing the video with a deck sales letter that is embedded straight on the page. This allows the consumer to consume the sales presentation at their own pace instead of being controlled by the video format.
The Process…
Step 1: Prepare the Deck Sales Letter - Create a mini webinar 1.0 or 2.0 depending on your business type that effectively communicates the value of your product or service. Ensure that the deck is visually appealing and easy to navigate.
Include a clear call-to-action on the final slide to apply below or to schedule a call below. Service businesses who prefer calls can place their phone number(s) on the final slide.
